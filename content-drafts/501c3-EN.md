# What 501(c)(3) status actually means

*A line in the US tax code, not a seal of approval. What an organisation had to do to get it, what it is worth to you as a donor, and the four things people reliably assume it proves when it does not.*

People say "501(c)(3)" when they mean "real charity". The shorthand works often enough to stick around and fails often enough to cause trouble. What the term actually names is a subsection of the Internal Revenue Code, the one the IRS grants federal tax exemption under. Whatever the status tells you comes from that. Nothing else comes from it at all.

The blurring tends to happen at the worst possible moment, when somebody is deciding whether to give. What follows is what the status demands, what it hands you, and the point where it runs out.

## What the organisation had to satisfy

The status goes to organisations "organized and operated exclusively" for one of the exempt purposes the section names: charitable, religious, educational, scientific, literary, testing for public safety, fostering national or international amateur sports competition, and preventing cruelty to children or animals. Purpose aside, three conditions do most of the work.

- No private benefit. The IRS puts it this way: "none of its earnings may inure to any private shareholder or individual". Making a surplus is fine. Handing it to insiders is not.
- No political campaigning. On intervening in a campaign for or against a candidate, the IRS calls Section 501(c)(3) organisations "absolutely prohibited". Lobbying on issues is allowed, within limits.
- Annual reporting to the IRS. Almost always on a form in the 990 series.

That is the entire test. What it leaves out is worth reading twice. Competence is not in there. Results are not in there. Neither is any measure of how much of a donation reaches the work.

## Public charity or private foundation

Every 501(c)(3) sits in one of two categories. The category changes what the organisation has to do, and it changes what you can deduct.

- A public charity is supported by the general public, by government, or by other public charities. Churches, hospitals, universities and the ordinary donation-funded organisations most people picture.
- A private foundation usually has one source of money behind it, often a family or a company, and tends to make grants instead of running programmes. Stricter rules apply to it, along with an excise tax on investment income.

For a donor the difference that bites is the deduction limit. Gifts to public charities can be deducted against a higher share of your income than gifts to private foundations. Both categories are public, and the IRS Tax Exempt Organization Search will tell you which one you are looking at.

## What it means for your donation

For a US taxpayer who itemises, a contribution to a 501(c)(3) is generally tax-deductible. That is the benefit most people are really asking after when they check the status, and it is genuine.

It is narrower than it sounds, though. How much you can deduct depends on the organisation's category, on anything you got back in return, and on your own tax position. Limits vary enough that the IRS assigns deductibility status codes, and its search tool shows the code for every organisation.

None of that says anything about quality. Deductibility describes your tax return. A well-run charity and a badly run one are equally deductible.

## The status can be taken away, quietly

Here is the part that catches people out, because nothing announces it. Miss the required annual return three consecutive years running and the exemption goes automatically. There is no hearing, and donors are not told. An automatically revoked organisation, in the IRS's words, "is not eligible to receive tax-deductible contributions".

- The loss is public and checkable. The IRS publishes the Automatic Revocation of Exemption list.
- Reinstatement can be applied for, and sometimes granted retroactively, so a name on that list is not automatically a dead organisation.
- The website, the brochures and the donation page will usually carry on as though nothing happened.

So the question worth asking is not whether they ever held 501(c)(3) status. It is whether they hold it today.

## What it does not mean

It does not mean effectiveness, or efficiency, or that anyone has ever looked at the results. Less obviously, it does not mean the organisation has filed anything you can actually read. Status and filing are two separate facts, and an organisation can hold the first while still owing the second.

> Four US charities in this catalogue carried a verified badge on tax-exempt status alone, with nothing filed behind it: the national Salvation Army office, Catholic Relief Services, Catholic Charities USA and Founders Pledge. Two of them had no filings of any kind on ProPublica, and the other two had filings that contained no data. Every one resolves, returns the right legal name, and opens when you click it. What opens is a registry record rather than a return. All four were unpublished.

## How to check it yourself

Four steps. None of them costs anything.

- Find the organisation's EIN, the nine-digit employer identification number, on its own website or on a donation receipt.
- Run it through the IRS Tax Exempt Organization Search. Check that the name matches, and read the deductibility code.
- Check the Automatic Revocation list in the same tool, or look for a revocation posting date, so you know the status is current and not historical.
- Open the most recent Form 990 and confirm it exists and carries figures. A return with no financial data in it tells you almost nothing.

## Common questions

**Is a 501(c)(3) the same as a nonprofit?**

No. "Nonprofit" is a corporate form granted at state level, while 501(c)(3) is a federal tax status. A body can be incorporated as a nonprofit and never obtain 501(c)(3) status, or hold it and lose it later. Different governments grant them, for different reasons.

**What is the difference between 501(c)(3) and 501(c)(4)?**

Both are tax-exempt, but only gifts to a 501(c)(3) are generally deductible for the donor. A 501(c)(4) social welfare organisation is free to campaign politically in ways a 501(c)(3) is not. Some groups run one of each under similar names, which confuses people constantly.

**Does 501(c)(3) status mean the charity is well run?**

No. The status examines purpose and structure, never performance. Nothing in the application or in the annual return asks whether the work achieves anything.

**The charity is not in the IRS search tool. Is it fake?**

Not necessarily. Churches do not have to apply for recognition, very small organisations may file nothing but the electronic postcard, and the tool's data is refreshed periodically rather than live. Absence is a reason to ask a question, not a verdict.

**How current is any of this?**

Less current than you would expect. The IRS publishes on a lag, and Form 990 data describes a fiscal year that has usually been over for some time. Across this catalogue, the median age of the newest available filing is 31 months.
